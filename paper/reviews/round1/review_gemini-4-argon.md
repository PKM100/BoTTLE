1. SUMMARY
This paper introduces BoTTLE, an open-source analytical framework and CLI tool that predicts the latency, energy, and monetary cost per successful robot task of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models on unowned target hardware. The core methodological contribution is an overhead-aware single-point calibration scheme that separates unexplained reference latency (attributed to Python/framework dispatch and host-side overhead) from roofline-predicted accelerator time, scaling the residual by relative host-CPU speed rather than accelerator FLOPs/bandwidth. Across 32 cross-device transfer pairs derived from 31 published batch-1 VLA latency measurements, overhead-aware calibration achieves 28.1% MAPE (vs. 48.8% for single-factor global calibration), and an application to LIBERO illustrates how task success rates, action chunking horizons, and robot/operator hourly rates alter model and device rankings.

2. VERDICT: Minor Revision — 7/10

3. STRENGTHS
- **Clear, high-value problem formulation:** Framing VLA evaluation around *cost per successful task* (incorporating action chunk execution horizon $H$, control frequency $f$, synchronous vs. asynchronous stalling, and $1/p$ expected retries) addresses a genuine gap between ML benchmark tables and real robotics deployment economics.
- **Simple, well-motivated calibration insight:** Identifying that eager-mode VLA inference on modern GPUs is heavily host/dispatch-bound (geometric mean $2.4\times$ the datasheet roofline, and up to $14\times$ for SmolVLA on H100 NVL) cleanly explains why naive multiplicative roofline calibration fails when transferring from datacenter GPUs to embedded Jetson platforms (63.3% MAPE on Jetson targets).
- **Honest reporting of the software-stack noise floor and negative/boundary results:** Section 4.2 explicitly documents that identical `(model, device)` pairs vary by up to $1.95\times$ across published studies due to software stack differences, and Section 4.1 candidly reports the 27% layer-extrapolation error and run-to-run variance on an unmanaged laptop.
- **Lightweight, reproducible artifact:** The shape-faithful synthetic profiler requires no multi-gigabyte model checkpoints or licenses, and all claims in the text and tables are backed by clean, inspectable code and JSON artifacts.

4. WEAKNESSES / THREATS TO VALIDITY
- **Anchor mismatch between eager-mode and compiled stacks in E3 (Section 4.3, Tables 2 & 3):** In `experiments/libero_cost.py` (`ANCHOR`), $\pi_0$ is anchored on the $\pi_0$ paper's RTX 4090 measurement of 73 ms, which Appendix A (`tables/dataset.tex`) explicitly notes is a **JAX** (XLA-compiled) implementation with 3 cameras, whereas all other five models are anchored on **eager PyTorch** H100 measurements (e.g., $\pi_{0.5}$ at 111.6 ms on H100 NVL from `arXiv:2611.06710`, OpenVLA-OFT at 42.4 ms, SmolVLA at 208.3 ms). Because $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$ share nearly the same 3.3B PaliGemma + flow-expert architecture, anchoring $\pi_0$ on a compiled JAX stack while anchoring $\pi_{0.5}$ on eager PyTorch creates an artificial $3.8\times$ latency gap on H100 (29 ms for $\pi_0$ vs. 111 ms for $\pi_{0.5}$ in Table 3, whose caption claims all numbers are `(ms, bf16, eager)`). This directly confounds the headline claim in Section 4.3 that "$\pi_0$ has the lowest predicted latency on 6 of 9 devices... but on none of them is it cheapest per success."
- **Asymmetric clipping in the overhead-aware calibration formula (Section 3.2, Eq. 3):** Equation (3), $\ell_t = \min(m/\hat\ell_r, 1)\hat\ell_t + \max(m - \hat\ell_r, 0)(\sigma_r/\sigma_t)$, assumes that whenever $m > \hat\ell_r$, 100% of the excess time is host-CPU overhead and the accelerator runs at exactly its default roofline efficiency ($\eta_c=0.5, \eta_m=0.75$). Conversely, when a slow or low-efficiency reference device has a loose roofline prediction or when transferring from a slower device to a faster one, this piecewise form behaves asymmetrically: for example, in `where-oft-fused` transferring from `a10g` ($m=204.1$ ms, $\hat\ell_r=150.6$ ms) to `h100-nvl` ($m=41.8$ ms), overhead-aware calibration predicts 83.6 ms (99.9% APE), whereas global calibration predicts 40.7 ms (2.6% APE) because the 53.5 ms gap on A10G was actually lower GPU compute/memory efficiency rather than host-CPU overhead. Similarly, in E1 (`transfer.json`), overhead-aware calibration is worse than global calibration when transferring `cpu-fp32 -> gpu-fp32` (39.2% vs. 33.4% MAPE) and `cpu-fp32 -> gpu-fp16` (30.3% vs. 18.9% MAPE). This failure mode (when the reference device is compute/bandwidth-bound with sub-default efficiency rather than host-bound) is not discussed in the text.
- **Unverified / extrapolated 2026 citations and hardware specs (References & Section 4.2):** The validation in E2 relies heavily on two 2026 preprints (`xu2026hwbench` arXiv:2610.34717 and `waseem2026where` arXiv:2611.06710) and hardware specs for RTX 5090, Jetson Thor, and Apple M5. While appropriate for the late-2026 submission context, several hardware parameters in `hardware.py` (e.g., `rtx-4090` and `rtx-5090` assigned `host_speed = 1.2` vs `1.0` for `h100-sxm`, and `t4` assigned `host_speed = 0.8`) deviate from the simpler 3-tier CPU-class prior stated in Section 3.2 ("1.0 for modern x86 server cores, 0.75 for Thor's Neoverse-V3AE, 0.45 for Orin's Cortex-A78AE", and Orin Nano's 0.35 is also omitted from the text).
- **Double-counting of `host_overhead_ms` inside `_total` calibration (Section 3.1 & `calibrate.py`):** In `analytical.py`, $\hat\ell = \sum_s t_s + h/\sigma$, where $h \in [4, 8]$ ms is a built-in host overhead prior (`host_overhead_ms`). When calibrating from an end-to-end measurement (`_total` in `calibrate.py`), `r_tot` and `t_tot` include `ref.host_s` and `tgt.host_s`, and `transfer()` then scales `t_tot` (which already contains `tgt.host_s` scaled by $\sigma_r/\sigma_t$) by $\min(m/\hat\ell_r, 1)$ and adds $\max(m - \hat\ell_r, 0)(\sigma_r/\sigma_t)$. While numerically minor when $m > \hat\ell_r$ (the $h/\sigma$ terms cancel in the sum), Equation (3) in the paper does not make clear whether $\hat\ell_r, \hat\ell_t$ include the prior $h/\sigma$.
- **Minor inconsistencies between text and tables/code:**
  - Abstract and Section 4.3 state "9 devices" are evaluated in E3, and `libero_cost.py` runs 9 devices (including `a10g`), but Tables 2 (`tables/cost.tex`) and 3 (`tables/latency.tex`) only display **8 device columns** (`a10g` is omitted from both tables without explanation).
  - Section 4.2 states "Spec-only predictions are within $2\times$ for all 31 cases", where "within $2\times$" is coded in `published.py` as `ape <= 1.0` ($|\hat\ell - m|/m \le 1$). However, for underpredictions, `ape <= 1.0` holds for *any* non-negative prediction $\hat\ell \in [0, 2m]$! In fact, $\hat\ell$ is $14\times$ smaller than $m$ on SmolVLA/H100-NVL (14.9 ms vs. 208.3 ms, ratio 0.071) and $6.5\times$ smaller on GR00T N1.7/H100 (15.6 ms vs. 101.5 ms, ratio 0.153). Calling a $14\times$ underprediction "within $2\times$" because $|\hat\ell - m|/m = 0.929 < 1.0$ is a mathematical error in interpreting APE.
  - In Section 4.3, SmolVLA's average success rate is written as "87.3%" in the prose, "87.2" in Table 2, and `0.8725` in `libero_cost.json`.
  - In `cost.py`, `achievable_hz` in `sync` mode is computed as `1.0 / (lat / horizon + 1.0 / task.control_hz)` (effective action rate), whereas `realtime_ok` in `sync` mode is hardcoded to `True`. Then in Section 3.3, the paper states that BoTTLE reports the Pareto front "over configurations that are real-time and fit in memory", but `pareto_front()` in `cost.py` does not filter by `realtime_ok` or `fits`.
