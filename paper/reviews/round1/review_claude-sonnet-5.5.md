## 1. SUMMARY
BoTTLE is a Python framework that predicts per-call VLA latency and energy on unowned hardware using a per-stage roofline model. It calibrates this from a single measurement with an "overhead-aware" rule: the part of the measurement the roofline cannot explain is carried across devices by host-CPU speed. It then converts latency into dollars per successful episode, accounting for chunking, sync/async execution and retries. On 32 cross-device pairs built from 31 published latencies, overhead-aware calibration gets 28.1% MAPE versus 48.8% for single-factor calibration. A LIBERO case study argues that the fastest model is not the cheapest per success and that the cost-optimal device flips with the cost regime. The engineering is useful and the framing (cost per success) is sensible. However, the validation rests on a small, non-independent, heterogeneous literature dataset with author-chosen priors. The LIBERO cost study is a derived calculation, not a validation, and several numbers in the text contradict the tables.

## 2. VERDICT
`VERDICT: Major Revision — 5/10`

## 3. STRENGTHS
- The problem framing is useful. Cost per successful task, with chunking, sync/async, retries and robot/operator time, is a clearer deployment metric than latency or success rate alone (§3.3).
- The paper observes and quantifies that eager-mode VLA latency is far above the roofline, e.g. the SmolVLA H100 NVL vs. Thor comparison (§4.2, "The roofline gap"). It also shows a concrete failure mode of naive calibration: single-factor calibration is worse than spec-only on Jetson targets (63.3% vs 54.0%).
- The paper is unusually candid about the software-stack noise floor (up to 1.95× for the same model and device) and states that a 28% error is near what the data can support. The advice to calibrate on the deployed stack is honest and practical.
- Baselines include "same latency as reference" and spec-only. A σ=1 ablation and a halved-Jetson-prior sensitivity check are reported.
- The paper reports the energy model's failure (107% MAPE with the default prior) instead of hiding it.
- The dataset of 31 published measurements with sources is a modest but reusable artefact (Appendix A). The tool is dependency-free and runs on a laptop.
- E1 is described honestly. It is explicitly demoted to a pipeline sanity check, and its measurement noise is acknowledged (SmolVLA fp16 measured 104 ms and 167 ms in two runs).

## 4. WEAKNESSES / THREATS TO VALIDITY

**Internal inconsistencies and apparent errors (credibility issues)**
- The abstract and §4.2 say the dataset covers "six VLAs." Appendix A lists eight distinct models: pi0, openvla, openvla-oft, gr00t-n1.5, n1.6, n1.7, pi05 and smolvla.
- The abstract says "nine devices." Tables 2 and 3 show only eight device columns, and the A10G, L40 and A100 do not appear in the cost study. The "6 of 9 devices" claim in the abstract and §4.3 therefore does not match Table 3, which has eight columns. In Table 3, π0 is lowest on H100, 5090, 4090, L4, T4 and Thor, which is 6 of 8 and not 6 of 9. Please reconcile the counts.
- §4.2 says spec-only predictions are "within 2× for all 31 cases." The same paragraph reports GR00T N1.6 at 23 ms measured vs. 3.2 ms roofline (7×) and SmolVLA at 208 ms vs. 15 ms (~14×). This contradicts the claim. A geometric-mean ratio of 0.41 together with "within 2× for all" is also implausible given those examples. The claim may have been computed against a different predictor (the "default efficiency" with the host term), but the text does not say so.
- The abstract says published latencies are "2.4× the roofline." §4.2 says the geometric-mean ratio of prediction to measurement is 0.41, which is also 2.4× (1/0.41). Please make clear whether this is the pure roofline or includes h/σ.
- Spec-only MAPE is 51.8% in Table 4 while the text says all 31 cases are within 2×. MAPE of 52% (under-prediction by 0.41 gives an APE of 59%) is not obviously compatible with every case being within 2×.
- Table 3 gives π0 on H100 as 29 ms and π0.5 as 111 ms, a 3.8× gap for architectures with the same action head and similar prefix size. This seems to come from anchoring each model on a different published source and stack (JAX-compiled π0 vs. eager LeRobot π0.5), not from model differences. The "fastest ≠ cheapest" claim is therefore partly a stack artefact. In §4.3, π0 is "fastest on 6 of 9 devices" largely because its anchor is a JAX-compiled measurement while others are eager. This is a confound in the headline result.
- The OpenVLA-OFT and GR00T numbers are anchored on whichever source was available, so the stack differs across models. The calibrated latencies in Table 3 are thus not like-for-like.
- The SR column in Table 3 shows 87.2 for SmolVLA, while §4.3 says 87.3.

**Validation of the central claim (E2)**
- The 32 pairs are not independent. Several are different targets from the same reference, share sources (the 2611.06710 tables supply most of the Thor/H100-NVL pairs), or repeat the same model/device with different settings. The "n=32" and MAPE figures carry no confidence intervals, bootstrap, or per-pair breakdown in the main text. A 28.1% vs 48.8% difference may well be significant, but this is never shown.
- The effective number of independent (model, target-device) cases is perhaps 10–15. Most of the gain comes from the Jetson targets, which are largely Thor with a handful of Orin points, and which are dominated by a single source study.
- The host-speed priors (1.0 / 0.75 / 0.45) are stated to be "not fitted to the validation data." But they are chosen by the author who had the data and the measured Thor/Orin ratios. The Orin 0.45 value is just 2–3 data points and is plausibly tuned. Beyond the σ=1 ablation and a halved-Jetson check, no real held-out test exists.
- The σ=1 ablation is revealing. With no host adjustment at all (31.2%), the method is already almost as good as the full method (28.1%). The main benefit therefore comes from treating the unexplained residual as a constant additive term instead of scaling it with accelerator speed. That is an almost trivial baseline ("measured − roofline = constant overhead"). The host-CPU story, which is the headline mechanism, is thus supported by a ~3-point difference with no significance testing. The paper should state this plainly. The title of contribution 2 and the abstract overstate the role of "host-CPU speed."
- Missing baselines: (a) fitting a two-parameter model on all non-reference data (e.g. a regression on log latency using FLOPs, bytes and a constant), (b) a pure additive-constant model, (c) a per-device empirical efficiency factor learned from the corpus. These would show whether the structure of the proposed calibration matters.
- Reference devices are always non-Jetson, so the claim of predicting "unowned embedded hardware" is tested in one direction only. Jetson→desktop and Thor↔Orin are never tested.
- The "within 25%" rate is 56%, so 44% of predictions miss by more than 25%. Abstract and discussion language ("sufficient for ranking") is stronger than this supports. The paper does not report ranking accuracy (Kendall τ, top-1 device agreement) on the predictions, which is what the ranking claims actually require.
- The prediction target is dominated by cross-source noise of up to 1.95×. This makes it impossible to tell a good model from a lucky one at 28% error. The paper acknowledges the noise floor but still presents the numbers as evidence of improvement. A within-source-only evaluation would help.
- The energy experiment uses 5 models from a single paper and a leave-one-out fit of one parameter. The 39% MAPE is on n=5, with no CI. The claim that "energy needs one calibration point" is not tested against any other device.
- E1 uses synthetic profiled transformers, not real VLAs. The conclusion that it "works" is weak (the paper itself notes the spread is comparable to the error). The profiler omits tokenization and preprocessing, which are exactly the host overheads the paper says dominate. E1 therefore cannot exercise the paper's own mechanism. Layer extrapolation adds 27% error for SmolVLA.

**Cost study (E3) is a model-derived calculation, not an empirical result**
- No cost is measured. All E3 numbers are outputs of the same model that carries ±30% error, combined with published LIBERO success rates (which come from different codebases, seeds and settings). The differences between models that drive the "fastest ≠ cheapest" claim are often small, e.g. π0 vs. GR00T N1.6 at 1.23 vs. 1.15 USD/1000 on Thor, or 0.68 vs. 0.59 on the 5090. These are well inside the ±30% uncertainty the paper itself states. Only the larger gaps (2–10×) are supported.
- The execution horizons for SmolVLA (10) and GR00T (8) are "assumed" and are not from published evaluations. Since H directly sets calls per episode, GR00T's advantage over π0 (H=5) in cost is driven in part by this assumption. The cost advantage of OFT and GR00T over π0 is therefore not independent of chosen H. Success rates were also measured at their own horizons, so the pairing is inconsistent.
- GR00T N1.6's 97.0% LIBERO figure and SmolVLA's 87.3% are cited without a source; they should be traced. Success rates are not conditioned on device or precision, and all models are run in bf16, which may not match how the published SRs were obtained.
- Success rate is assumed independent of latency, but under synchronous control in a real robot, latency affects success. LIBERO is simulated and pauses for the policy, so the "robot cost" and "operator cost" terms are hypothetical. The paper notes this only in Discussion. The headline conclusion that the optimal device flips from Orin Nano to H100 is therefore driven almost entirely by the chosen $5/h and $35/h rates, which are not justified or sensitivity-tested. A flip point (the robot $/h at which the cost-optimal device changes) should be reported.
- The "operator" regime at $35/h with the H100 optimal for "three of six models" is a narrow result (Table 3: H100 wins for OpenVLA, OFT and π0, by margins like 125 vs 135, which is within the noise). The abstract presents the flip to H100 as a headline finding.
- Device cost comparisons mix very different pricing models (amortized purchase at 50% duty vs. cloud hourly), and H100 vs 5090 amortization at a fixed 3 years/50% duty is arbitrary. Off-board compute ignores network latency, which is a first-order cost for an H100 serving a robot. Thus the H100 "winning" is not credible without a network term. Sync latency is also what makes H100 attractive, and it is exactly the setting where network RTT would matter.
- The Orin Nano "cheapest by 2.5–4× in compute only" result is an artefact of the cost model, because robot and operator cost are removed and device cost is only amortized purchase. The finding is nearly tautological: cheap device, low $/h.
- The success-at-60%-of-max-length assumption is only tested for model ranking on Thor, not for the device flip.
- "Long horizon costs 2.4–4.3× more than Spatial" is largely a function of the maximum episode lengths (520 vs. 220 steps, 2.4×) and success rates. It is nearly arithmetic and not an insight.
- No real validation of the cost output: no one measures actual task time, energy or cost on any device for any model. The end-to-end cost per success, the paper's headline quantity, has never been compared to a measurement. Even a single real LIBERO run on one available device would help.

**Scope, novelty and presentation**
- Novelty is modest. A roofline plus a constant residual is well known, and the paper's own σ=1 ablation shows most of the gain comes from that. The task-level cost model is standard accounting. The paper's claim that "no prior work combines…" is not substantiated beyond a short related-work paragraph (e.g. no comparison with LLM simulators such as Vidur/LLMCompass-style tools, or with MLPerf-style cost/perf metrics).
- Many device peaks (A10G, Thor, Orin) and some model dimensions (GR00T N1.6/N1.7, SmolVLA) are approximate, as the paper concedes. The roofline-vs-measurement gap in these cases is therefore confounded with spec error.
- The paper leans heavily on very recent preprints and README numbers (Isaac-GR00T README FAQ), so the dataset is not peer-reviewed and cannot be re-checked for batch size, sequence length or camera count. Settings differ across rows (cameras, wrist/proprio, MAXN, fused attention) and are not controlled.
- A single author, a single experimental machine, and no released raw measurement logs for E1 beyond the repo.
- Duplicate rows with the same model/device but different settings (e.g. OFT/H100-NVL 42.4 vs 41.8, Thor 181/180.6/250) inflate the 31 count and the pair set.

## 6. REQUIRED FIXES FOR ACCEPTANCE
1. **Fix the numerical and textual contradictions.** (writing-only) Reconcile "six VLAs" vs. eight in Appendix A, "nine devices" vs. eight columns, "6 of 9" vs. 6 of 8, and "within 2× for all 31" vs. the 7× and 14× examples. Reconcile the SmolVLA SR of 87.2 vs. 87.3. Define exactly which predictor the 0.41 geometric-mean ratio refers to.
2. **Add statistical rigor to E2.** (NEW-EXPERIMENT, cheap analysis on existing data) Provide bootstrap confidence intervals clustered by source/model/target, a paired test of overhead-aware vs. global calibration, and a per-pair table. Report the number of independent (model, device) cases. Report ranking accuracy (Kendall τ / top-1) of the predictions, since the paper's use case is ranking.
3. **Add the missing baselines and a held-out evaluation.** (NEW-EXPERIMENT) Add an additive-constant calibration (σ=1) as a named baseline and a learned regression baseline. Run a held-out test where the host priors are fixed before seeing the data, or fit by leave-one-source-out. Add Jetson→desktop and Thor↔Orin transfer. State plainly that most of the gain does not depend on the host-speed priors, and soften the host-CPU narrative accordingly.
4. **Control the stack confound in E3.** (NEW-EXPERIMENT or re-analysis) Anchor all models on the same stack/source class (e.g. all from the 2611.06710 eager measurements) or report both. Show that the "fastest ≠ cheapest" result survives, especially given that π0 is anchored to JAX-compiled latency. Propagate the ±30% (or per-pair empirical) error into the E3 cost and report which rankings and flips are robust (e.g. Monte Carlo over latency error, horizon and success rate).
5. **Sensitivity and flip-point analysis for the cost regimes.** (writing plus cheap analysis) Report the robot/operator $/h and the device-price/lifetime/duty at which the optimal device changes. Include a network-latency term for off-board GPUs or restrict the H100 claim. Justify $5/h and $35/h. Test the assumed horizons (SmolVLA 10, GR00T 8) and the 60% fraction against the device flip, not only the Thor model ranking.
6. **Validate cost per success against at least one real measurement.** (NEW-EXPERIMENT) Even a small run on the available device (e.g. a real VLA such as SmolVLA on the M5, or on Colab T4) comparing predicted vs. measured time and energy per episode. As it stands, the headline quantity has no empirical validation.
7. **Tone down claims.** (writing-only) The abstract's "cost-optimal device flips…" and "no hardware purchase required" should be qualified as model outputs with ±30% uncertainty under assumed prices. Remove the claim of "sufficient for ranking" unless item 2 supports it. Replace "novel" language with a precise delta against the closest prior work, including LLM inference simulators.
8. **Provenance of the dataset and success rates.** (writing-only) Give citations for the GR00T N1.6 and SmolVLA LIBERO success rates and exact settings (batch, cameras, precision) for each latency row. Release the machine-readable table and scripts. Flag the duplicated or setting-varied rows.
9. **Strengthen or de-emphasize E1.** (writing-only, or NEW-EXPERIMENT) Either include the host-side preprocessing/tokenization in the profiled pipeline or state clearly that E1 cannot test the paper's mechanism.

The single most important blocker is #4 together with #6. The headline cost-per-success conclusions are produced by the model alone, with differences that are mostly inside its stated error. They also rest on anchors from different software stacks (compiled JAX vs. eager), so the "fastest ≠ cheapest" and device-flip findings are confounded and unvalidated. The E2 improvement (#2, #3) is real but under-evidenced statistically.

## 7. QUESTIONS FOR THE AUTHORS
1. How many of the 32 pairs are independent after accounting for shared reference/target/source? What is the bootstrap CI on 28.1% vs. 48.8%, and what is the per-pair breakdown?
2. Since σ=1 gives 31.2% MAPE, what exactly does the host-speed prior add? Was the Orin value of 0.45 chosen before or after seeing the Orin measurements (which are only a few points)?
3. Does "fastest ≠ cheapest" hold if π0 is anchored to an eager PyTorch measurement instead of the JAX-compiled 73 ms? What are the numbers for π0 and π0.5 on the same source?
4. Where do the GR00T N1.6 (97.0%) and SmolVLA (87.3% vs. 87.2%) LIBERO success rates come from, and under what horizon? How much of GR00T's cost advantage over π0 comes from the assumed H=8 vs. 5?
5. Does the H100 optimum under the operator regime survive a network round-trip term (even 5–20 ms) in synchronous mode?
6. How do you reconcile "within 2× for all 31 cases" and a 0.41 geometric-mean ratio with the 7× and 14× examples, and with the 51.8% MAPE?
7. Is the 25% within-rate (56%) and the ±30% uncertainty enough to support the ranking claims? What is the top-1 device agreement between predicted and measured?
8. Have you measured an actual end-to-end task (time, energy, dollars) on any device and compared it with BoTTLE's prediction?
